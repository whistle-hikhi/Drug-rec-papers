# Human Factors & Clinician-in-the-Loop

Added 2026-09-08 to close the gap flagged in [`NOTES.md`](../../NOTES.md): the collection was entirely model-side — nothing on how clinicians actually use, trust, override, or get overwhelmed by these systems in practice. This category is about the *human* side of deployment: trust and automation bias, alert fatigue and override behavior, explainability/acceptance, and implementation science. Almost none of it is specific to *medication recommendation* architectures from `01-ehr-based`/`04-llm-generative` — it's drawn from the much larger clinical-decision-support (CDS) literature generally, with medication-alert-specific and prescribing-specific papers called out wherever found. Read this category as "what happens after a model like GAMENet or FLAME ships," not as more recommendation methodology.

**Headline finding**: unlike the fairness gap, this one is *not* a search artifact — human-factors research on medication-related CDS is a mature, decades-old sub-field (alert-override studies go back well before deep learning). The real gap is the opposite direction: almost none of this literature evaluates the *specific* GAMENet/SafeDrug/LLM-generation-style recommendation systems from `01-ehr-based` and `04-llm-generative` — it studies today's rule-based/alert-based CDS. Whether clinicians trust, override, or experience alert fatigue from a *combinatorial ML recommender* (as opposed to a single-drug interaction pop-up) appears to be genuinely unstudied.

## B. Alert fatigue & override behavior

The classical medication-safety human-factors literature — mostly about single-drug/DDI pop-up alerts in CPOE systems, not combinatorial recommenders, but the empirical base every "safety-aware" model in `01-ehr-based`/`02-ddi-aware` implicitly assumes clinicians will act on.

| Year | Paper | Venue | Key idea | Link |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2020 | Machine Learning Approach to Reduce Alert Fatigue Using a Disease Medication-Related Clinical Decision Support System: Model Development and Validation | JMIR Medical Informatics | Uses ML to *triage* which medication alerts are worth firing at all — a rare example of ML applied to the human-factors problem itself, not just the recommendation problem | [paper](https://medinform.jmir.org/2020/11/e19489/) |
| 2022 | Appropriateness of Alerts and Physicians' Responses With a Medication-Related Clinical Decision Support System: Retrospective Observational Study | JMIR Medical Informatics | Sobering headline number worth citing directly: of 382 alert cases evaluated, only **7.3% were judged clinically appropriate** — a concrete measure of how noisy medication alerts are in practice | [PMC9579928](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9579928/) |
| — | Tackling Alert Fatigue with a Semi-Automated Clinical Decision Support System: Quantitative Evaluation and End-User Survey | Swiss Medical Weekly | Combines quantitative override-rate measurement with an end-user survey — pairs the behavioral data with the subjective experience behind it | [paper](https://smw.ch/index.php/smw/article/view/3357) |
| 2023 | The Overriding of Computerized Physician Order Entry (CPOE) Drug Safety Alerts Fired by the Clinical Decision Support (CDS) Tool: Evaluation of Appropriate Responses and Alert Fatigue Solutions | PMC | Reports the field's standard headline statistics directly: **90-96% of alerts overridden overall; drug-interaction alert override rates exceeding 85%** — the empirical backdrop that motivates every DDI-safety claim in `02-ddi-aware` | [PMC9754301](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9754301/) |
| 2026 | Challenges to Reduce Alert Burden Using Current Decision Support Infrastructure in Two Commercial EHR Systems: Lessons Learned and Path Forward | PMC | Documents the *infrastructure* ceiling — even well-designed alert-reduction efforts are constrained by what two major commercial EHR platforms will actually let you configure | [PMC12196563](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12196563/) |

## Notes for later reading

- **Start with section E's first entry (PMC13386419)** — it's the only paper found that synthesizes trust, alert fatigue, nursing vigilance, *and* equity/governance specifically for medication safety, and its CSIL framework is a ready-made lens for organizing everything else in this category. Read it first, then use sections A-D to go deeper on whichever theme it raises that matters most for your framing.
- **The nursing-vigilance point is easy to miss and worth flagging**: almost every other paper in this collection (across all seven categories) is framed around the physician or the model — PMC13386419 is the only one that names nurses as a distinct, "often-overlooked" safety layer. If your system's deployment story involves a hospital ward, this is a gap in the *system* design, not just in this literature collection.
- **The 90-96% alert-override statistic (section B) is worth citing directly whenever motivating why "DDI-safety-constrained" isn't automatically "safety-achieved"** — a model in `01-ehr-based`/`02-ddi-aware` can hit a low DDI rate on paper and still fail in deployment if clinicians have already learned to ignore its warnings the way they've learned to ignore CPOE pop-ups.
- **Real gap, not a search artifact**: none of the papers here study clinician response to a *combinatorial ML medication recommender* (GAMENet/SafeDrug/FLAME-style) specifically — they study single-alert CPOE/CDS systems, or general-purpose diagnostic/treatment AI. A human-factors study of how clinicians actually respond to a full prescribed-set recommendation (not a single interaction warning) looks like genuine open territory, and would bridge this category directly back to `01-ehr-based` and `04-llm-generative`.
- **Automation bias (section A) and alert fatigue (section B) are two failure modes pulling in opposite directions** — over-trust (accepting bad AI output uncritically) vs. under-trust (ignoring good AI output because of noise) — and a deployed medication recommender has to avoid both simultaneously. Worth stating this tension explicitly if human factors becomes part of your framing, rather than treating "clinician trust" as a single axis to maximize.
- **Cross-reference with `05-fairness-bias`**: the "equitability" trust factor named in PMC9136656 (section A) and the equity gap named in PMC13386419 (section E) both point back at the fairness literature — clinicians appear to treat *fairness* itself as a trust-relevant property of a system, not a separate concern.
